Finding 1

The best-evidenced exercise cannot be delivered by an app

Zhu et al. (2023), network meta-analysis, 44 studies, 1,757 participants.

Open-skill activity ranks first for executive function — football, table tennis, badminton.

1.96SMD, 95% CI 1.15–2.77 · SUCRA 98.0%
“require participants to react in a dynamically changing and externally paced environment” Zhu et al., defining open-skill activity

The active ingredient is an opponent moving unpredictably in physical space.

A phone cannot be an opponent.

The key move: the thing that makes it work is the thing software can't supply. Not a limitation of effort or budget — a structural mismatch. If they ask "couldn't an app simulate external pacing?" — that's exergaming, and slide 5 answers it.

Finding 2

The app-shaped option was the only one that failed

“all types of physical exercise interventions except exergaming were superior to non-physical exercise controls” Zhu et al. (2023)

Exergaming — physical and cognitive training combined, gamified, on a screen — is the category that most resembles the app I was planning. It is the one intervention in the network that did not beat doing nothing.

3 of 59 studies. No effect size reported for it.

Finding 3

In adults, the evidence barely exists

  • Dinu et al. (2023), 159 adults, the best-powered adult attention trial located — “There were no effects of exercise on attention, cognitive or motor impulsivity, or movement in those with ADHD.”
  • Mehren et al. (2019) found reaction times improved — measured 9.8 minutes after the bike stopped, range 7–16. The only later measurement was an imaging probe at 33 minutes, and it was null: “implicating a limited duration of exercise effects.” No cognitive measure was taken after about 26 minutes.
  • Xu, Zhao & Hu (2026) on chronic exercise in adults — “the limited number of studies precluded quantitative synthesis.”

A fourth review reported a large chronic effect (SMD −1.77). Read in full, it pools three studies and 105 participants — one null, one non-significant. It does not contradict the above.

evidence-table.md §2, §3, §6, §8
6
The 9.8 minutes is the detail that lands hardest — people assume "exercise improves focus" means for the day, not for twenty minutes. Be precise if pushed: the 33-minute probe was an fMRI measurement of brain activation, not a cognitive task. The honest claim is that the study cannot speak to persistence beyond about 26 minutes, and its own imaging check at 33 was null. Verified against the paper 2026-10-04. If asked about the -1.77 paper, the breakdown is in the evidence table: three studies, 105 participants, one null yoga result, one Tai Chi CI spanning zero.

What the trials do agree on

Adherence, not exercise selection, is the constraint

33%of Svedell's intervention group dropped out before attending a single session

None dropped out after starting. All the attrition sat at initiation — the point where planning and initiation are required, and the point no exercise protocol addresses.

“It has not been possible to exercise on my own at all… Even though I felt motivated, it didn't work.” Participant, Svedell et al. (2023)

Authors attribute it to “difficulties with planning and organization.”

This is the pivot. Motivation was present and execution still failed — that's an executive-function problem, not a motivation problem, and it's the distinction the whole project now rests on.

The gap

A trial registered the fix — and never delivered it

The START trial's protocol registered three arms. The second would deliver occupational-therapist-led training in “time management skills, planning and organization.”

The published trial had two arms. Eleven of 43 participants received the training as an ad-hoc fix and were pooled into the exercise group.

“a confounding factor… that could not be isolated in the results” Axelsson Svedell et al. (2025), on their own abandoned arm

Their own protocol: whether such training “can contribute to increased physical activity or to maintaining routines for this” has not been studied.

The artifact

What I'm building

A scheduler for adults with ADHD. Daily tasks with alerts and reminders; weekly and monthly goals broken down into daily actions; completion logging; and follow-up when something is missed, rather than the task silently disappearing.

It maps onto what the trials actually provided

What trials supply with staff

Scheduled appointments · SMS and email reminders · make-up rules for missed sessions · minimum attendance floors · an occupational therapist for planning and time management

What the app supplies

Scheduling · alerts and reminders · catch-up prompts on a missed task · completion tracking · goal decomposition into daily actions

Why this isn't just another to-do app: it implements only the components the review finds load-bearing, and it is judged on whether it delivers them reliably — not on how many features it has.

Scope discipline

What this project does not claim

  • Not attention. Not focus. Not symptoms. Far transfer from training to general cognition is approximately null in the literature — it is where claims like mine go to die.
  • Not a treatment or a diagnostic. A support tool, framed as one.
  • Not tested on people. IRB approval needs CITI training, a faculty PI, and four weeks' lead time. Experimental work must finish 13 November. Not feasible — and collecting human data without approval is misconduct, not a shortcut.

The measurable objects are the literature and the instrument — not the users. That is what makes this answerable this semester.

Where this could go wrong

Three risks I can see

1   The trials might not report what I need.

My analysis needs trials to say how much support they gave people and how many dropped out. Two I have read do not. START promised to measure attendance and published nothing.
If that happens: it becomes the finding — “trials that call adherence the main problem do not report adherence data” is a real result. It changes what the paper is about, so I want to know in October, not November.

2   Too few trials, or far too many.

Exercise trials in adults with ADHD alone may number fewer than twenty — not enough to show a pattern. Widen it to every behavioural intervention and it could be hundreds, at roughly half an hour to code each one.
What I'll do: write the inclusion criteria down before searching, so the scope is a decision rather than whatever the search returns.

3   Building the app could eat the research time.

A scheduler with reminders and goal tracking is real software. It would be easy to spend six weeks polishing it and reach 13 November with nothing measured.
What I'll do: the review comes first and decides what gets built. The app only has to do what the evidence supports — fewer features, not more.
